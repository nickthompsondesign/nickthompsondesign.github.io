Treasured Possession: Instant Quote Flow

Turning a long single-page price form into a guided, multi-step quote that shows the price from the first choice and gives the business a way to follow up with every customer.

In progress: research and design stage

The Brief

Treasured Possession cleans and preserves wedding dresses for customers across the UK. Their online price form asked customers to complete every field before showing a price, and the business had no way to follow up with people who left. The client asked us to make getting a price as easy as possible and to turn quote requests into contactable leads.

My Role

Lead designer. I planned and ran the research, audit and competitor benchmarking, and designed the flow, wireframes and the visual design. I created the look and feel in Figma, building on the brand's existing website, then turned it into a working prototype. A developer at Infoserve built it. The client set pricing and business rules.

What success looks like
  • More quote-page visitors completing an order
  • A contactable lead for every quote, even when the customer doesn't buy
  • Fewer people dropping out before they see a price
Constraints
  • Fixed agency budget for design, build and testing
  • WooCommerce with an existing product-options plugin
  • Small client team handling orders and follow-up
  • Analytics access had to be restored before we could set a baseline

The Challenge

An "instant price" you had to work for

The page promised an instant price, but customers had to work through every section of the form, including two required dates, before pressing "Calculate Order". That button was actually WooCommerce's add-to-basket button with a new label, so the first time a customer saw their total was in the basket.

The product-options plugin was already working out a running total as customers made their choices. The theme hid it. Anyone who left before the basket left no trace, and the business had no way to follow up.

Heuristic evaluation

I reviewed the live form against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics, clicking through every option to map the conditional fields. Each issue is tagged with the principle it breaks and a severity rating.

Annotated audit of the original form (scroll the image). Numbers match the findings below.

  • 1
    The order summary never showed a price. The "Your Order" panel kept its placeholder text however many options were chosen. HighVisibility of system status
  • 2
    "Calculate Order" added the item to the basket. The label described one action and the button did another. HighMatch between system and the real world
  • 3
    Order type and dress style couldn't be chosen with a keyboard. The radio buttons behind the picture cards were hidden in a way that removed them from the tab order. HighWCAG 2.1.1 Keyboard
  • 4
    Pressure instead of help on dress value. Customers were told to "ensure" they chose the correct band, with no guidance if they weren't sure. MediumError prevention
  • 5
    Placeholder-only text fields. The designer and special-details boxes had no visible label, and the hint disappeared once you started typing. MediumRecognition rather than recall · WCAG 3.3.2
  • 6
    Quantity boxes hidden until ticked. The copy asked for a quantity "in the boxes", but the boxes only appeared after ticking a circle. MediumConsistency and standards
  • 7
    Rules as small print. The 6pm cut-off for next-day collection was a note, not built into the date picker. LowError prevention
  • 8
    An ambiguous required field. "Delivery date" could mean the wedding date or when the dress should come back. HighMatch between system and the real world
  • M
    No summary or reassurance on mobile. On phones, the order panel and customer reviews sat below the Calculate button, out of sight for the whole form. MediumVisibility of system status

Accessibility audit

An automated axe scan against WCAG 2.2 AA flagged only one issue: two icon links with no text. Manual checks found the problems that matter most for this form. The service and style cards can't be reached by keyboard (finding 3), the two text areas and both date fields have no programmatic label.

The gap between the two results shaped the plan: every new component would be checked by hand with a keyboard and screen reader, not just with automated tools.

What it told me: every competitor shows a total within two clicks, and none asks for an email first. That mattered because one of the client's goals was to capture emails: today they have no visibility of people who leave without buying, so no way to follow up on abandoned quotes. The research showed that asking for an email before the price adds effort before customers know whether they can afford it, so I chose to capture it later, as a way to save the quote, rather than as a gate at the start. Re:Finery proved a value-band model can still feel instant. Elsie May showed that a single line of reassurance can take the stress out of a pricing choice.

User Flow

Splitting the journey by what's being sent

The old form assumed every customer had a wedding dress. The new tool starts with one question, "What can we help with?", with three answers: clean a wedding dress, clean other garments, or just a preservation box. Customers without a dress skip the dress questions entirely, and can still add one later without losing anything.

The journey is split into two parts: get a quote, then book. Customers see their price before being asked for dates, so the quote is useful even to people who aren't ready to book.

  1. Your dressStyle first, then value bands priced for that style
  2. Anything else to clean?Extras with quantity steppers, most popular first
  3. Returning your dressBreathable bag, own box or a preservation box sized from the dress
  4. Your preservation boxOnly if a box was chosen: pattern, live preview, and extra boxes for other items
  5. Your quoteItemised total, saved and emailed
  6. BookCollection date, delivery speed and an optional "need it back by", then review

Design Decisions

Each change answers a finding

Price on every option, from the first step · Findings 1, 2

Every card shows what it costs, and a running total follows the customer through the flow (a sticky bar on mobile, a sidebar on desktop). Nobody has to reach the basket to find out the cost. The client reports that around 80% of visitors are on mobile, so the flow was designed for phones first.

Reassurance instead of pressure on dress value · Finding 4

The value step explains in one line why it asks: "Choose the band closest to what you paid. It sets your price and how your dress is covered." It removes the warning tone, each band shows its price, and the band is checked when the dress arrives. Style, value and optional details sit in three labelled cards on one screen, so the step feels short.

Confirm with the clientThat they will check the band on arrival and contact the customer before any change.
Quantity steppers for extras · Finding 6, order data

Additional items use – 0 + steppers, matching the instruction to choose a quantity. They're listed in order of popularity, led by the veil. The first version hid the less popular items behind "Show more"; testing showed people preferred to see everything, so all items now show.

Delivery chosen on a colour-coded calendar · Findings 7, 8, order data

Customers pick a DPD collection date, then a return delivery date on a calendar in the style of hotel booking sites. Every return date is labelled and coloured by what it costs: Standard (+£18, back in around four weeks) or Express (+£50, back within 10 to 12 days), with a key underneath. Delivery is one price or the other, never both. Only 4% of orders chose Express, so Standard is the default and Express sits beside it as the faster option, rather than being pushed. Studio drop-off is still free, but it sits behind a quiet "Prefer to drop it off in person?" link because the client would rather collect.

Email capture framed as saving the quote · "No second chance"

The quote page offers one clear choice: "Book now, or save for later". The email is needed for both, so it comes first, and the save button carries a save icon and a quiet "Price held for 30 days". After saving, customers see where the quote was sent with a "Change email" link, in case of a typo. The same email powers the resume and abandoned-cart emails.

Choosing a box by sight, sized for you · Review themes

The current box page lists five patterns as text, with no pictures, and uses one photo for two sizes. Six of the 13 reviews praise the packaging, so it matters. The new box step sizes the box from the dress style already given (A Line → Regular, for example), selects the most popular pattern, shows all five as swatches, and updates a large packshot as the customer chooses.

Returns grouped by what customers are choosing · Testing

Return options are split into "Boxed and preserved" and "Bag options". Each name says who supplies it: "Our preservation box", "Your own box, packed by us", "Your own bag" and "Our breathable bag". The preservation box is marked "Most popular" and lists its benefits as short ticks, with the full explanation one tap away.

A review step that reads as one story · Testing

The review page is a set of numbered cards: your order (with an Edit button and edit links on every line), an optional phone number "so we can easily keep you updated about your order", what happens next as four illustrated steps, and the terms. Help is always one call away in the side panel.

Accessible by default · Finding 3, 5

Cards are real radio buttons with visible focus, every field has a visible label, and price changes are announced to screen readers.

Look and feel

I designed the visual style in Figma so the quote feels like part of the Treasured Possession site rather than a bolted-on form. It uses the brand's plum and blush, Poppins, and the blush circle that sits behind headings on the live site. I drew a new set of fine line icons for every garment, bag and box, and used rounded pill shapes for cards, buttons and tags so choices feel soft and easy to tap.

Where to ask for the email

The client's first instinct was to ask for an email before showing any prices, to capture leads. None of the competitors do this, and it adds effort before customers know whether the service is affordable. We compared two versions:

Version A: email first

Customers enter their email before seeing any prices.

Version B: price first

The quote shows first, and the email saves it, with a promise to hold the price for 30 days.

With around 23 orders a month, a live A/B test on orders would take months to give an answer. Instead, we agreed to launch price first, framing the email as "Save your quote" so it offers something in return. Every saved quote is recorded with its own status in WooCommerce, which shows how many quotes become orders and gives the client leads to follow up. Email first stays ready to test against it.

Agreeing changes with the client

I took the updated prototype to the client, Imran and Hamza, to review the testing findings and settle the open questions. We agreed:

  • 1
    "Own box" becomes a service. Renamed "Pack and present with your own box", so the £30 is clearly for expert packing with acid-free tissue. The client is recording a short video showing the cleaning and packing process.
  • 2
    Fewer charges that feel like penalties. The admin fee on orders without a wedding dress is removed, and the breathable bag is shown as included rather than as a £10 add-on.
  • 3
    Clear delivery choices. Standard at £18 with a four-week return, or Express at £50 with a 10 to 12 day return, marked on the calendar. It's one price or the other, not both.
  • 4
    A follow-up for saved quotes. If a customer doesn't book, an email after 7 days offers 10% off, and a reminder after 20 days comes before the 30-day price hold ends.
