Treasured Possession: Quote Form Redesign

A redesign of the wedding dress cleaning quote form. The new form shows the price from the first step and saves each quote as a lead. I built the working prototype with Claude Code, and I'm working with a developer to turn it into a WordPress plugin.

Designed, prototyped and tested. Launch results to follow.

The Brief

Treasured Possession cleans and preserves wedding dresses across the UK. They could see people starting the price form and not finishing it, and once someone left there was no way to contact them. They asked us to make getting a price as easy as possible, and to turn every quote into a lead they could follow up.

My Role

Lead designer. I ran the research, designed the flow and visuals in Figma, and built the working prototype with Claude Code. A developer at Infoserve is turning it into a WordPress plugin.

What success looks like
  • A price with less effort
  • A lead for every quote
  • Follow-up emails and a discount for people who don't book
Constraints
  • Fixed agency budget for design, build and testing
  • WooCommerce with an existing product-options plugin

The Problem

Getting a price was hard work

The form was confusing, and customers only saw a price once they reached the basket.

The "Calculate Order" button was WooCommerce's add-to-basket button with a different label. The form was already calculating the price as options were chosen, but the theme hid it. The business had no contact details for anyone who left before the basket, so it couldn't follow up.

Research

What the research found

The price was hidden, some questions could be answered from order data, and competitors show a price within two clicks.

With a small budget, I chose methods that each answered a specific question: an audit of the live form, a year of order data and a benchmark of three competitors, backed up by customer reviews and a quick usability test with colleagues.

1Audit of the live form

The price was hidden, and parts failed accessibility checks

I marked up the live form against usability heuristics and WCAG 2.2. The numbers match the image.

  1. 1
    The order summary never showed a price. The "Your Order" panel kept its placeholder text.
  2. 2
    "Calculate Order" added the item to the basket. The label said one thing and the button did another.
  3. 3
    Order type and dress style couldn't be chosen with a keyboard. An automated scan missed this; checking by hand found it.
  4. 4
    Pressure instead of help on dress value. Customers were told to "ensure" they chose the right band, with no guidance.
  5. 5
    Text fields had no labels. The hint disappeared once you started typing.
  6. 6
    Quantity boxes were hidden until ticked. The copy asked for a quantity before the box appeared.
  7. 7
    Rules were in small print. The 6pm cut-off for next-day collection wasn't built into the date picker.
  8. 8
    "Delivery date" was ambiguous. It could mean the wedding date or when the dress should come back.

The Insight

Three principles for the redesign

The findings led to three principles: show the price early, ask fewer questions, and give customers a reason to share their email.

  • 1
    Show the price from the first choice. Every option shows what it costs, and a running total follows the customer through the flow.
  • 2
    Ask only what changes the price. Required questions before a price dropped from five to two: dress style and value.
  • 3
    Earn the email by saving the quote. Instead of a gate at the start, the email saves the quote and holds the price for 30 days. If the customer doesn't book, a follow-up email after 7 days offers 10% off, and a reminder arrives before the price hold ends.
The New Flow

A new flow based on what the customer is sending

The first question decides which steps each customer sees, and the quote is shown before any dates are asked for.

The old form only worked for wedding dresses. To get a groom's suit cleaned, a customer had to order a wedding dress clean first and add the suit as an extra. I saw this as a possible bottleneck. The client said they rarely get those orders, but that could be because the form doesn't allow them.

We agreed to add the option and use the order data after launch to see whether it's worth pursuing. The new form starts with "What service?": clean a wedding dress, clean other garments, or just a preservation box. Customers see their price before being asked for dates, so the quote is useful even to people who aren't ready to book.

Testing

Changes made after testing

I tested a working prototype with real prices. It showed three places where people hesitated, and the client agreed the changes.

I built the wireframes into a prototype that calculated real prices, then tested it with colleagues from outside the project, each working through a scenario on a phone or desktop.

Testing the prototype with colleagues.

What we foundSome testers weren't sure which style their dress was, and the price didn't move until a value band was chosen.
What changedA "Not sure?" panel helps people choose, and each style shows its "from" price, so the total updates straight away.
What we foundThe plain date picker didn't show what each date meant for delivery speed.
What changedA calendar in the style of hotel booking sites marks every return date as Standard (+£18) or Express (+£50), by colour and label.
What we found"Why does it cost £30 to use your own box?" People couldn't see what they were paying for.
What changedRenamed "Pack and present with your own box", so it reads as an expert packing service.

Price first, not email first

The client's first instinct was to ask for an email before showing any prices, to capture leads. None of the competitors do this, and it adds effort before customers know whether they can afford it.

A: Email firstCustomers enter their email before seeing any prices.
B: Price firstThe quote shows first, and the email saves it, holding the price for 30 days.

With around 23 orders a month, an A/B test would take months, so we agreed to launch price first and frame the email as "Save your quote". Every saved quote is recorded in WooCommerce, so the client can see how many become orders and follow up the rest.

Final Design

The finished quote form

The tested flow in Treasured Possession's brand style, built as a working prototype for desktop and mobile.

I designed it in Figma using the brand's plum and blush, Poppins and a new set of fine line icons, then built it as a clickable prototype with real prices so the client and developer could try every route before build. It's designed to WCAG 2.2 AA, with keyboard-friendly cards, visible labels and price changes announced to screen readers.

What's Next

What we'll measure

The form hasn't launched yet. Eight weeks after launch, these measures will be compared with the same period last year.

  • 1
    Quote-page visitors who place an order, before and after.
  • 2
    Contactable leads captured from saved quotes, where previously there were none.
  • 3
    Orders recovered by the follow-up emails for saved quotes and abandoned baskets.

The new flow also opens up ideas the client wants to explore next: seasonal box designs, suit preservation as its own service, and preservation bought as a gift.
